Chinese Exclusion Act (1882): The first major federal law to restrict immigration based explicitly on race and nationality. It banned Chinese laborers from entering the U.S. for 10 years and prohibited Chinese immigrants from attaining naturalized citizenship. Immigration Act of 1882: Formally shifted immigration authority to the federal government. It levied a federal "head tax" on non-citizens entering the country and barred the entry of convicts, individuals with mental illnesses, and anyone deemed likely to become a "public charge".

Alien Contract Labor Laws (1885 and 1887): Banned American employers from importing foreign workers under pre-arranged labor contracts, aiming to protect domestic wages.

Scott Act (1888): Expanded the Chinese Exclusion Act by making it illegal for Chinese residents who temporarily left the United States to return.

Immigration Act of 1891: Established the first comprehensive system of federal immigration enforcement by creating the federal Bureau of Immigration. It also authorized the deportation of unauthorized immigrants and added polygamists and people with contagious diseases to the excluded list.

Geary Act (1892): Extended the Chinese Exclusion Act for another 10 years and required all Chinese residents to carry internal residence certificates or face deportation or forced labor.

Immigration Act of 1903 (Anarchist Exclusion Act): Expanded inadmissible categories to include anarchists, political extremists, and people suspected of human trafficking.

Immigration Act of 1907: Increased the head tax and allowed the president to enter into informal restrictions, leading to the "Gentlemen's Agreement" that severely capped Japanese immigration.

Immigration Act of 1917 (Asiatic Barred Zone Act): Overwhelmingly restricted immigration from a designated geographic zone across South and Southeast Asia. It also instituted a mandatory literacy test for all adult immigrants. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]

Emergency Quota Act of 1921: Responding to post-WWI isolationism, it set the first numerical caps on immigration. Annual limits for any nationality were restricted to 3% of that foreign-born population living in the U.S. during the 1910 census. [1, 2]

Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed Act): Tightened restrictions significantly by dropping the quota to 2% and moving the baseline back to the 1890 census. This calculation favored Western and Northern European immigrants while strictly choking off entry from Southern/Eastern Europe and outright banning immigration from Asia. It also authorized the creation of the U.S. Border Patrol. [1, 2, 3]

Magnuson Act (1943): Repealed the Chinese Exclusion Act to honor the WWII alliance with China, allowing a small annual quota of Chinese immigrants and granting them naturalization rights. [1, 2]

Displaced Persons Act of 1948: Offered a humanitarian workaround to the strict quota system, allowing hundreds of thousands of European refugees displaced by World War II to resettle in the U.S.

Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 (McCarran-Walter Act): Unified all existing immigration laws into a single framework (Title 8 of the U.S. Code). While it formally ended outright racial bans on naturalization, it kept the restrictive national-origins quota system intact and added severe vetting filters for suspected communists or subversives. [1, 2]

Immigration and Nationality Act Amendments of 1965 (Hart-Celler Act): A monumental shift that completely abolished the national-origins quota system. It replaced quotas with a preference system built around family reunification and specialized job skills, paving the way for modern immigration demographic patterns.

Refugee Act of 1980: Separated refugee admissions from standard immigration channels. It aligned the U.S. definition of a "refugee" with international United Nations standards and established the modern statutory framework for processing political asylum seekers.

Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 (IRCA): Addressed unauthorized immigration through a two-pronged strategy: it instituted criminal penalties for employers who knowingly hired undocumented workers (introducing the I-9 verification form) while simultaneously granting legal amnesty to roughly 2.7 million undocumented immigrants already residing in the country.

Immigration Act of 1990: Raised the overall annual ceiling for legal immigration, established the modern Diversity Visa Lottery, and introduced new employment pathways, including the H-1B visa for high-skilled technical workers and the EB-5 investor visa. It also codified Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for individuals escaping war or natural disasters.

Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA): Greatly enhanced border enforcement, streamlined the fast-track deportation process ("expedited removal"), and imposed multi-year entry bans for individuals who accumulate periods of unlawful presence in the U.S.

Homeland Security Act of 2002: In the wake of the September 11 attacks, this law dissolved the legacy Immigration and Naturalization Service (INS). It divided federal immigration duties into three new agencies under the newly formed Department of Homeland Security (DHS): USCIS, ICE, and CBP.

REAL ID Act of 2005: Established strict federal security standards for state-issued driver's licenses and identification cards, requiring proof of legal residency status for standard airport security access.

Secure Fence Act of 2006: Authorized the construction of hundreds of miles of double-layered tactical fencing along the U.S.–Mexico border alongside advanced surveillance technology
