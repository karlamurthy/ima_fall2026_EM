Chapters 1 & 2 The Society of the Spectacle by Guy Debord, 1967

The word spectacle is really interesting to me, how its meaning has shifted over time, first rooted in the Latin specere, meaning "to look at" or "to observe" If you look up on google, it states the following.

Mid-14th Century: Entered the English language from Old French and Latin (spectaculum), meaning a public show, a grand display, or an object of wonder and curiosity.
Late 14th Century: Extended to describe a single vision-assisting device.
Early 15th Century: Shifted to the plural form spectacles to mean glass lenses mounted in a frame worn on the face to help people see.
Modern Era: Grew to include critical theory meanings, such as Guy Debord’s 1967 concept of the "society of the spectacle," where media and consumption replace real lived experience.
I was suprised to find this week’s essay included in the word’s evolution!

“The spectable is not a collection of images but a social relation among people mediated by images.” As I was reading the essay, the specatacle almost took on an autonomy, as if it were a cognizant living thing itself, perhaps in the same way we sometimes talk about AI and its recursive self improvement models. “The spectacle wants to get to nothing other than itself.”

As with last weeks essay, this is also incredibly contemporary. “Coniditions of isolation of ‘lonely crowds”, today's lonely epidemic caused by our phones. I’m reminded of a photograph of a crowded subway filled with people with their heads in their newspapers, anonymous and intimate.

“The spectacle is the moment when the commodity has attained the total occupation of social life.” So here we are. Complete alienation from and by these new fangled gadgets. But yet, through these same technologies, we see a rips in the spectacle, a story of suicides by factory workers in China, an image of a child in Bangladesh rummaging through e-waste.

It’s not just consumerism, and capitlaism, it's also the selling of a culture of convenience as writen about in Tyranny of Convenience by Tim Wu and by other writers. Are we headed to a Wall-e like future? I see resistance everywhere, the park crowded with people having a picnic on a beautiful summer day, the thrifting rage by kids (including my own), mutual aid societies, the Amazon Labor Union..

As We May Think by Vannevar Bush, 1945

The very last paragraph could have come from an article today about the development of AI over its dangers. Ominous “He may perish in conflict before he learns to wield that record for his true good.” But yet he argues that it would be an “unfortunate stage” (spoken like a true scientist) at which to “terminate the process or to lose hope as to the outcome.” His faith in scientific progress feels contemporary, but fails to fully grasp the context of our capitlistic market driven society.

Vannevar Bush was an American engineer and inventor. He was projecting a future that we live in today, technology that stores, processes, recognoizes patterns in vasta amounts of data, memory, records, all interconnected, and searchable. He was imagining a landscape of thumbdrives and data centers. Science (non)fiction.

The “memex” “unlike memory, they would never fade.” But how can that be possible? All it takes is the powergrid to go down, some glitch in the matrix, a hack. The “memex” is incredibly fragile, like the i cloud world we live in today.

I love seeing this article within the conext of the advertisements. Reminiscemnt of a few National Geographics I have from the 1920s, which contain ads promoting the saftey of lead paint for your baby’s room and toys, and show images of doctor’s smoking cigarettes, alongside cruises to “exotic” faraway places. How far we’ve come, but not.

The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction by Walter Benjamin,1935

This was written three years before the beginning of World War 2, during the rise of the Nazi regime. Benjamin was a German-Jewish philosopher, and knowing this history changes the way I read the epilogue. When I first encountered it, it felt like a huge turn in the essay. But knowing the historical context, I now read it differently. He was writing on the brink of catastrophe. He was confronting a world in which technological progress, mass media, and political violence were becoming increasingly intertwined. Sounds like the moment we’re in today.

Benjamin traces the transformation brought about by mechanical reproduction and how it changes our understanding of authenticity, authority, and what he calls the “aura” of an artwork. I wasn’t entirely sure, at first, what he meant by aura. It seems partly connected to an artwork’s uniqueness, the fact that it exists in a particular place and time, with a particular history. I was thinking of aura like “punctum,” but not sure that’s what he is referring to. I think as a filmmaker, I would like to see aura defined more as that relationship between viewer and the work, punctum.

I was also interested in Benjamin’s comparison between the painter and the cameraman. He writes that “the painter maintains in his work a natural distance from reality, the cameraman penetrates deeply into its web.” My oldest sister was a painter, and when she would paint me, I very much felt this. She seemed to be penetrating deeply into my “web” observing me. There was an intimacy to being painted that I found difficult to escape. Interestingly, my experience with a camera has often been the opposite. Holding a camera can actually give me distance and a sense of safety from whatever I am filming, particularly when I am making personal films. The camera becomes a kind of buffer between me and the subject. I can look through it rather than directly at something.

I’ve been thinking about this metaphor a lot while making my most recent film. One of the biggest inspirations for that work was the research of Stanford memory scientist Dr. Charan Ranganath. His work has made me think about memory as being more like painting than photography. We don't simply retrieve a fixed photograph of the past. Each time we revisit a memory, we can reconstruct it differently, painting it again based on who we are in that particular moment. And the process of painting informed my process of filmmaking.

What strikes me most about Benjamin’s essay is how familiar some of his anxieties feel today. There seems to be a recurring tendency, whenever a new technology emerges, to imagine that it will lead to the destruction of culture, attention, or even society itself. Benjamin was writing about mechanical reproduction, photography, and film. Today, I find myself thinking about AI, TikTok, and the ever-growing demand to produce more and more “content” that can be distributed instantly to anyone, anywhere, often stripped of context. More distraction. Less contemplation. More passive consumption. More “asocial” behavior. “quantity” being transformed into “quality.”

Are we at another inflection point like the one Benjamin was witnessing? And yet, Benjamin also seems to say that mechanical reproduction.. Or the power of films can be politicized too to mobilize the masses. Although I’m curious about if his view? I wasn’t sure. Propaganda films of his era. Rampant fake news alongside images of Gaza from Palestinians in ours. Benjamin was watching one technological transformation unfold at the edge of catastrophe. I suppose we are watching another unfold now.

In Defense of the Poor Image by Hito Steyerl, 2009

So much of technology has evolved since this essay was written in 2009. Poor images today can now masquerade as rich images, because of new technologies like Topaz to upres old footage. In my last project, I had to constantly tell my color corrector not to make all my old super 8, vhs and mini dv footage look too clean and perfect. I wanted to keep all the imperfections, the digital hits, the film grain. Or there is now an affinity for adding in these artifacts, pixilating, blurring, VHS effect on footage.

The definition of poor images today perhapsmust rely more on what Steyerl says about how it is shared - the swarm circulation, the digital dispersion, as opposed to the actual quality of the image. Did the phrase “going viral” exist in 2009? Curious what it means in the age of memes, the commodfication of poor images - what is reality. Are poor images still worth defending? Or should there be a different definition of poor images?

I was also really struck by this quote “Focus is identified as a class position, a position of ease and privilege, while being out of focus lowers one’s value as an image.” To have the privilege to think about focus in one’s life, means to have options, to have some sort of economic security and freedom. Similar to a piece I made around the word "free" and how the word changes depending on what or who it's adjacent to.
